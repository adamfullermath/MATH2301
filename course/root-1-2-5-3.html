    1.

    Calculate the following limits.
    🔗

    (a)

    \(\lim_{x\rightarrow -3^+}\frac{x+4}{x+3}\)
    Solution.
    Note that we cannot plug in \(x=-3\) into \(\frac{x+4}{x+3}\text{,}\) because then we would be dividing by \(0\text{.}\) As \(x\) gets close to \(-3\text{,}\) \(x+4\) approaches \(1\text{.}\) As \(x\) gets close to \(-3\text{,}\) \(x+3\) approaches \(0\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\rightarrow -3^+} \left|\frac{x+4}{x+3}\right| =\infty. \end{equation*}
    Note that, as \(x\) approaches \(-3\) from above, \(x+3\gt 0\text{,}\) and \(x+4\gt 0\text{.}\) Hence
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\rightarrow -3^+} \frac{x+4}{x+3}=\infty. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \(\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}\frac{3x^2+x-7}{x^2 + x -2}\)
    Solution.
    We will divide top and bottom by the highest power of \(x\) in the denominator. Here, that is \(x^2\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}\frac{3x^2+x-7}{x^2 + x -2} \amp = \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}\frac{(3x^2+x-7)\frac{1}{x^2}}{(x^2 + x -2)\frac{1}{x^2}}\\ \amp = \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} \frac{3 + \frac{1}{x}-\frac{7}{x^2}}{1 + \frac{1}{x}-\frac{2}{x^2}}\\ \amp = \frac{\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}3 +\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty} \frac{1}{x} - \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}\frac{7}{x^2}}{\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}1 + \lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}\frac{1}{x}-\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}\frac{2}{x^2}} \\ \amp = \frac{3+ 0 + 0}{1+0+0}=3. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Suppose that a ball is dropped from the upper observation deck of the CN Tower, \(450\)m above the ground. The equation of motion of the ball is given by
    \begin{equation*} s = f(t) = 4.9 t^2. \end{equation*}
    Find the velocity of the ball after \(5\) seconds. (Use limits to calculate your answer).
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    The velocity at the time \(t = 5\) is given by
    \begin{equation*} v(5) = \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{s(5+h)-s(5)}{h}. \end{equation*}
    Note that \(s(5) = (4.9)(25)\) and \(s(5+h)=4.9(25+10h+h^2)\text{.}\) Thus
    \begin{align*} v(5) \amp= \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{4.9(25+10h+h^2)-4.9(25)}{h} \\ \amp = \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{4.9h(10+h)}{h}\\ \amp = \lim_{h\rightarrow 0} 4.9(10+h) = 49\ \text{m/s}. \end{align*}
    Note that, in this problem your solution should include units: meters per second m/s.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    This is an alternate approach to the same question. Instead of directly calculating \(v(5)\) we can find the general formula for \(v(t)\text{,}\) and then evaluate at \(t=5\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} v(t) \amp = \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{s(t+h)-s(t)}{h} \\ \amp = \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{4.9(t^2 +2th + h^2)-4.9t^2}{h} \\ \amp = \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{4.9h(2t + h)}{h} \\ \amp = \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}4.9(2t + h) = 9.8t \text{ ft/s}. \end{align*}
    Hence, the velocity at \(t=5\) seconds is
    \begin{equation*} v(5) = (9.8)(5)=49\text{ m/s}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Let \(f(x) = x^2 - x.\) Use the limit definition of the derivative to find a formula for \(f'(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The formula for the derivative of \(f\) is
    \begin{equation*} f'(x) = \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}. \end{equation*}
    Hence
    \begin{align*} f'(x) \amp = \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{(x+h)^2-(x+h)-[x^2-x]}{h} \\ \amp = \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{x^2 + 2xh +h^2 -x-h-x^2+x}{h} \\ \amp = \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\frac{2xh+h^2-h}{h} \\ \amp = \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}(2x + h - 1) \\ \amp = 2x -1. \end{align*}
    Hence \(f'(x) = 2x - 1.\)

    4.

    The graph of a function \(f(x)\text{,}\) with \(x\) in the interval \([-4,4]\) is given below. Use the graph to answer the following questions about the derivative \(f'(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (a)

    On what interval(s) is \(f'(x)\) positive?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The derivative \(f'(x)\) gives the slop of the tangent line to the curve \(f(x)\) at \(x\text{.}\) Thus, \(f'(x)\) is positive when \(f(x)\) is increasing. From the picture \(f(x)\) is increasing up until \(-2\) and \(f(x)\) starts increasing again at \(x =2.\) Thus, \(f'(x)\) is positive on
    \begin{equation*} (-4,-2) \cup (2,4). \end{equation*}
    If we assume that \(f(x)\) continues to decrease before \(x-4\text{,}\) and \(f(x)\) increases after \(x = 4\text{,}\) we could say that \(f'(x)\) is positive on
    \begin{equation*} (-\infty, 2) \cup (2,\infty). \end{equation*}
    I accepted either answer as correct.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    On what interval(s) is \(f'(x)\) negative?
    🔗
    Solution.
    By the same reasoning as in part (a), \(f'(x)\) will be negative where the graph of \(f(x)\) is decreasing. From the picture, \(f(x)\) is decreasing between \(x = -2\) and \(x=2\text{.}\) Thus, \(f'(x)\) is negative on the interval.
    \begin{equation*} (-2,2). \end{equation*}
